Why Ketoacidosis is Rare in Type 2 Diabetes

One of the most common board questions involves distinguishing DKA (Diabetic Ketoacidosis) from HHS (Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State). While Type 1 diabetics are prone to DKA, Type 2 patients rarely develop it. Why?

The Role of Residual Insulin

The key lies in the mechanism of lipolysis inhibition. It takes very little insulin to suppress Hormone Sensitive Lipase (HSL) in adipose tissue. Type 2 diabetics, despite having insulin resistance, usually produce enough endogenous insulin to block HSL.

Without active HSL, free fatty acids are not released in massive quantities, and the liver does not produce ketone bodies (acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate) at a rate that overwhelms the buffering capacity of the blood.

Clinical Implications

This is why Type 2 patients present with HHS: severe hyperglycemia (>600 mg/dL) and dehydration, but normal pH and bicarbonate levels. Treatment focuses on aggressive hydration rather than insulin loading.

Research Update • Genetics

CRISPR 2.0: The Rise of Prime Editing

Traditional CRISPR-Cas9 creates double-strand breaks (DSBs), which can lead to unintended indels. Prime Editing, a newer technique, uses a "nickase" to cut only one strand.

This allows for "search-and-replace" gene editing without the risks associated with DSBs. Recent studies suggest this could correct up to 89% of known pathogenic human genetic variants.
